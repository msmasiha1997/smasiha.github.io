Sampling from Manifolds of Minimizers

From loss and gradient evaluations to sampling an entire solution set.

Saeed Masiha·18 October 2026·Interactive explainer

Suppose a smooth loss g(x) has a curved set of minimizers. We can evaluate the loss and its gradient, but cannot parametrize the set or project onto it. How can we sample a distribution that explores the whole solution set?

Sampling settings that work for one loss may deteriorate as the loss changes. Can one sampling prescription work across an entire family?

We start with Gibbs concentration and Langevin sampling. Our Superquantile–Gibbs (SQ-G) paper [1] establishes uniform guarantees: under its structural, regularity and tail assumptions, common choices of temperature, step size and run length control concentration and sampling errors across the family.

01 / Start with one loss

The minimizer set and local growth.

S=arg minx∈Rdg(x),g⋆=minxg(x)

Every point in S has the same loss. Local quadratic growth describes how loss rises away from the set:

g(x)−g⋆≥m2dist2(x,S)near S.

Here m > 0; dist(x, S) is the distance to the nearest minimizer. Nearby, small excess loss means proximity to S. It does not explain how to explore the set.

Where local PL enters

The local Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition relates the gradient to excess loss:

12‖∇g(x)‖2≥α(g(x)−g⋆)

For α > 0 near S, local PL prevents the gradient from being too small relative to excess loss. Together with the paper’s smoothness, tail and global structural assumptions, it yields a compact, connected C2 minimizer manifold with positive-definite loss Hessian in normal directions.

This geometry supports a Gibbs limit and concentration near S, alongside low-temperature Poincaré control for Langevin mixing. ULA additionally needs the step-size and initialization conditions discussed below.

02 / Concentration

Cool the distribution toward the valley.

At temperature λ > 0, the Gibbs law assigns more density to lower loss:

μλ(dx)=1Zλe−(g(x)−g⋆)/λdx

Zλ normalizes the density. Decrease λ below: probability gathers in a thinner band around the minimizers.

A quantitative guarantee.

For fixed losses satisfying SQ-G’s geometric, smoothness and tail conditions, the Gibbs law approaches a law μ0 supported on S, with

W1(μλ,μ0)≤Kλ

For sufficiently small λ. W₁ is the minimum average distance needed to transport one probability law into another.

Conditions and references for this bound

SQ-G, Proposition 2.10, uses the paper’s PL∘ condition, a bounded second fundamental form of S, and specified smoothness and tail bounds.

The rate uses Assumption 2.6 and Assumption 2.7, together with four clauses of Assumption 2.8: a C2 extension, a globally Lipschitz lower-level gradient, a continuous Hessian, and the uniform tail gradient error bound.

The full PL∘ condition in Definition 2.2 also includes C4 regularity near local minima, boundedness of the set of local minima, and a negative-definite Hessian at critical points outside the local PL neighborhoods.

The Laplace/Wasserstein reference used in SQ-G is Hasenpflug, Rudolf and Sprungk (2024), Theorem 3.6 [2]. SQ-G adapts their argument to its geometric assumptions: Proposition 2.9 identifies the limit and Proposition 2.10 gives the fixed-loss W₁ bound.

Which distribution lives on the minimizer set?

An ellipse makes the next comparison easy to see: move the same distance away from two minimizers and compare the loss.

The limiting law need not be uniform along the set. Where the loss rises more gently as we leave the set, more nearby points have low loss. These regions receive more limiting probability per unit of manifold volume.

dμ0dvolS(x)∝det(HNg(x))−1/2

HNg is the Hessian restricted to normal directions. It measures how sharply the loss rises perpendicular to the set. This differs from the curvature of the set itself. In this toy ellipse, the limit is uniform in the ellipse’s parametric angle, not in arc length.

03 / Computation

Now sample the Gibbs law.

Hold λ fixed. The unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA) combines a gradient step with Gaussian noise:

Xk+1=Xk−h∇g(Xk)+2hλξk

h is the step size; ξk are independent standard Gaussian vectors. The gradient pulls toward low loss, while noise lets samples explore. The update needs no parametrization of S, projection or normalizing constant.

Reaching the solution set is only the first step.

Samples can get close to S while remaining clustered in one region. To approximate the Gibbs law, they must also explore other regions in the right proportions. A Poincaré inequality helps control how quickly the underlying Langevin diffusion approaches this distribution:

Varμλ(φ)≤CPI∫‖∇φ‖2dμλ

For a smooth test function φ, this compares variance with average squared gradient. At fixed λ, a smaller CPI gives a stronger relaxation bound for the diffusion.

Yun Gong, Niao He and Zebang Shen [3] establish a temperature-independent bound on CPI for sufficiently small λ in their local PL setting, with additional regularity, tail and geometric assumptions. Their result relates this bound to the minimizer manifold’s spectral gap.

ULA also needs smoothness, controlled initialization and jointly chosen sampling parameters; a fixed step leaves discretization error. The experiments use ellipse/circle losses with globally Lipschitz gradients, distinct from the opening Gibbs examples.

The sufficient conditions and the cooling tradeoff

ULA needs gradients. Function values alone require an additional gradient estimator, which is outside this illustration.

SQ-G, Proposition 5.6, uses a Lipschitz gradient for G = g/λ, a Poincaré bound, and finite order-three Rényi divergence of the initial law relative to the Gibbs target. It jointly chooses a horizon and step to control order-two Rényi error, which yields a W₁ bound using Poincaré control and finite second moments.

The theorem’s step for G is λh in our convention. It does not justify arbitrary initialization or indefinitely increasing the iteration count at any fixed step.

For the corresponding continuous Langevin diffusion, finite initial χ² divergence gives decay by a factor exp(−2λt/CPI). With temperature-independent Poincaré control, the bound gives a timescale proportional to 1/λ, apart from initialization and accuracy factors. Cooling sharpens the target but slows exploration; this exact diffusion rate is not a finite-step ULA rate.

Tune two errors, not one.

Let νn,h,λ be the law after n ULA steps. The triangle inequality separates the computational and temperature errors:

W1(νn,h,λ,μ0)≤W1(νn,h,λ,μλ)+Kλ
  1. Temperature: choose λ ≤ (ε/2K)² within the theorem’s low-temperature range, so the second term is at most ε/2.
  2. Sampling: choose an admissible initialization, step size h and count n together, so the first term is at most ε/2.

The endpoint law is then within ε of μ0 in W₁; in particular, its expected distance to S is at most ε. With the paper’s controlled Gaussian initialization, the bound is polynomial in inverse target accuracy, with dimension- and problem-dependent constants. The example schedules above illustrate the tradeoff; they are not certified ε-budgets or guarantees for arbitrary nonconvex losses.

04 / Our results: a parameter family

One sampling prescription for every θ.

Return to the opening question: settings that work for one loss may deteriorate for another. The constants can grow, or the temperature range where a bound holds can shrink.

Uniform control means common bounds and parameter choices work across the whole parameter set. This concerns the guarantees; the limiting law can still give different weights to different minimizers.

Let θ range over a compact parameter set Θ, and define:

S(θ)=arg minxg(θ,x)

For each θ, μθλ is the Gibbs law and μθ0 its limit on S(θ). SQ-G establishes the common controls below under parameter-uniform assumptions.

What must hold uniformly?

The local PL condition now reads ½‖∇xg(θ, x)‖² ≥ α(g(θ, x) − g⋆(θ)), with a common positive α in the controlled neighborhoods of S(θ). Here g⋆(θ) is the minimum at that parameter.

SQ-G assumes this parameter-uniform local PL structure, smoothness and tail control over compact Θ. Its uniform Poincaré result concerns positive-dimensional, compact connected minimizer manifolds. The proof establishes common geometric neighborhoods and spectral bounds; a pointwise PL or Poincaré statement alone is insufficient. Assumptions 2.6–2.8 and the uniform Poincaré proof.

Sampling also requires controlled initialization and a jointly chosen step size and run length, as specified in the sampling conditions. The example schedules illustrate the mechanism; they are not theorem-calibrated accuracy guarantees.

First: uniform Gibbs concentration.

SQ-G, Proposition 3.10, gives a common constant K and a common positive temperature threshold:

supθ∈ΘW1(μθλ,μθ0)≤Kλ

One temperature choice controls the entire family. Within the common temperature range, the same K√λ bound applies to every θ.

Second: uniform sampling control.

Proposition 3.11 gives one finite inverse Poincaré bound and one low-temperature window valid across Θ:

CPI(θ,λ)≤CPI⋆∀θ∈Θ

Together with uniform smoothness and initialization bounds, this allows a common choice of ULA step size and run length at a given temperature and accuracy. SQ-G’s sampling construction makes these choices explicit.

The takeaway is uniform control of both errors. Choose λ to control concentration for every θ, then choose h and n to control sampling for every θ:

supθ∈ΘW1(νn,h,λθ,μθ0)≤ε

The guarantee concerns runs at each fixed θ with admissible initialization and sampling parameters. Tracking a continuously changing target is a different problem.

05 / A brief application

Sampling supplies the candidates.

In bilevel learning, θ can represent training-data weights and x a model. Sampling lets us examine different training minimizers using validation loss. Select-then-differentiate [4] pursues optimistic selection; SQ-G uses an upper-tail average to approximate pessimistic selection.

The previous article explains the data-reweighting application and LLM experiments. Those use practical optimizer branches; this article’s simulations isolate the mathematical sampling problem.

The toy losses and what the figures compute

The opening Gibbs animation uses wavy and bent loops. A smooth shear of an ellipse defines each loss; its density and limiting law are computed directly. These examples illustrate Gibbs concentration and are not used for the ULA simulations.

The normal-rise illustration and ULA experiments use gθ(x) = (qθ(x) − rθ²)²/(1 + qθ(x)), where qθ is a positive quadratic form around a translated center. Their minimizers form ellipses or circles; θ changes the center and radius. The fixed-loss examples hold θ = 0.5. This separate family has globally Lipschitz gradients; the sheared examples need not.

Numerical quadrature supplies the Gibbs densities and reference clouds. ULA uses analytic gradients and seeded Gaussian noise, without projection or a normalizing constant. Known geometry is used only for drawing and diagnostics. These illustrative computations do not certify a theorem’s full-distribution error bound.

One accuracy target. A sampling prescription for the whole family.

Under the structural, regularity and tail assumptions, with controlled initialization, common choices of λ, h and n control both approximation errors across θ. This makes sampling useful for minima selection without requiring a parametrization of the solution sets.
